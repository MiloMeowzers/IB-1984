How to use this guide

References take the form 2.9 (40%): Part Two, Chapter 9, about 40 per cent of the way through the chapter, rounded to the nearest 5 per cent. “App” is the Appendix, “The Principles of Newspeak”. This works in any edition.

Built for Paper 2. The issue arcs trace each concern through the novel’s three parts, and Dig deeper sets out readings to test against it.

The examination

Course and paperIB Diploma English A: Language and Literature, Paper 2: the comparative essay on two literary works studied in the course. Paper 2 is not a set-text exam: the questions are general, they do not name any work, and there are no extracts.
Format1 hour 45 minutes at both SL and HL. Answer one of four general questions, comparing two of the literary works you have studied. 25 marks.
CriteriaA Knowledge, understanding and interpretation (5). B Analysis and evaluation (10), in two strands: B1 how meaning is shaped by language, technique, style and broader authorial choices (5); B2 the similarities and differences between the two works (5). C Focus and organisation (5). D Language (5).
What this means for revisionYou will not know the question in advance, so you cannot prepare a single essay. You prepare a small number of moments from each work that you know in detail, grouped by issue, and you rehearse how each work handles those issues differently. That is what the issue arcs, the event bank and the quote bank in this guide are for. Comparison must be structural: every paragraph needs both works, and criterion B2 assesses it directly.
iSWIM for Paper 2. Use the five guiding questions on every prompt.
iWhat is the issue of significance? The concern beneath the prompt’s topic word.
SWhy does it matter? The human, social or philosophical stakes.
WWhat does each author want you to understand about it?
IHow does each author get you to understand it? Structure, form, characterisation, voice, staging.
MWhat does each work position its audience towards, and what does the comparison reveal? The essay-level M comes from the tension between M(A) and M(B).

Write each paragraph with PEACE ACT: Point, Evidence, Analysis, Comparison, Extension, Audience, Context, Tie-back. Characters do not exist: reach through the characters to the choices the writer makes with them.

iSWIM tells you how to plan. Thinking with iSWIM tells you how to think. Your text-type guide tells you what to notice and what to call it.

Context

Facts about the writer and period are attributed to a named source. Facts about the work come from the work.

Composition and publication

Orwell wrote the novel in the late 1940s, partly on the island of Jura, while seriously ill; it was published in 1949, and he died in January 1950.

Source: Biographical record (e.g. D. J. Taylor, Orwell: The Life, 2003)

Totalitarianism

The novel draws on Orwell’s knowledge of Stalin’s Soviet Union and Nazi Germany, and on his experience of propaganda during the Spanish Civil War and at the BBC during the Second World War.

Source: Biographical record; Orwell, “Why I Write” (1946)

Event bank

17 numbered events in order. Read one chapter for close revision, or E1 to E17 straight through as the arc of the whole work.

Chapter 1.1

E1 A bright cold day 1.1 (0–5%)

The novel opens with clocks striking thirteen 1.1 (0%) and posters announcing surveillance 1.1 (5%).

Why it matters. The ordinary is made strange from the first sentence.

Dig deeper. What is unsettling about a clock striking thirteen?

E2 The diary and the Hate 1.1 (45–95%)

Winston starts a diary, attends the Two Minutes Hate against Emmanuel Goldstein 1.1 (55%), and finds he has written “DOWN WITH BIG BROTHER” over and over 1.1 (95%).

Why it matters. The first act of rebellion is writing.

Chapter 1.2

E3 The Parsons and the place with no darkness 1.2 (5–95%)

Winston helps his neighbour Mrs Parsons, whose children play at being spies 1.2 (5%). He remembers a voice in a dream promising a meeting in the place where there is no darkness 1.2 (60%). He knows he is already a dead man 1.2 (95%).

Why it matters. The family has become an arm of the state.

Chapter 1.3

E4 Controlling the past 1.3 (70%)

Winston reflects on the Party slogan about the past 1.3 (70%) and on doublethink 1.3 (70%).

Why it matters. The novel’s central mechanism: history is whatever the Party says it is.

Chapter 1.4

E5 The Records Department 1.4 (5–85%)

Winston rewrites old newspapers and drops originals into the memory hole 1.4 (5%); he invents a war hero, Comrade Ogilvy, who never existed 1.4 (85%).

Why it matters. Winston is a writer of lies, and good at it.

Dig deeper. Is Winston an opponent of the Party or one of its craftsmen?

Chapter 1.5

E6 Syme and Newspeak 1.5 (0–25%)

Syme, a philologist, explains that Newspeak is meant to narrow the range of thought 1.5 (25%).

Why it matters. Language is the Party’s deepest weapon.

Chapter 1.7

E7 The proles and two plus two 1.7 (0–100%)

Winston writes that if there is hope it lies in the proles 1.7 (0%), and that freedom is the freedom to say two plus two make four 1.7 (100%).

Why it matters. Freedom is defined as the right to state the obvious.

Chapter 1.8

E8 Mr Charrington’s shop 1.8 (80%)

In the prole quarter Winston buys a glass paperweight with coral inside 1.8 (80%) from Mr Charrington and learns a fragment of the rhyme “Oranges and lemons” 1.8 (75%).

Why it matters. Objects and rhymes from the past become symbols of a world the Party has erased.

Chapter 2.1

E9 Julia’s note 2.1 (5–25%)

A girl from the Fiction Department 2.1 (5%) slips Winston a note reading “I love you” 2.1 (25%).

Why it matters. Love arrives as an act of rebellion.

Chapter 2.2

E10 The Golden Country 2.2 (65–100%)

Winston and Julia meet in the countryside, a place he recognises from dreams 2.2 (65%). Their sex is a political act 2.2 (100%).

Why it matters. Desire becomes resistance.

Dig deeper. Is love in the novel private, or always political?

Chapter 2.8

E11 O’Brien’s flat 2.8 (45%)

O’Brien receives them and they swear to do anything for the Brotherhood, including throwing sulphuric acid in a child’s face 2.8 (45%).

Why it matters. The rebels commit themselves to the Party’s own methods.

Chapter 2.9

E12 Goldstein’s book 2.9 (10–55%)

Winston reads Goldstein’s book, which explains the structure of Oceanian society 2.9 (10%); the best books, he thinks, tell you what you already know 2.9 (55%).

Why it matters. The theory confirms but does not change anything.

Chapter 2.10

E13 The arrest 2.10 (45–100%)

Winston and Julia say “We are the dead”; an iron voice from a hidden telescreen replies 2.10 (50%). The paperweight is smashed 2.10 (75%), and Mr Charrington is revealed as the Thought Police.

Why it matters. Their private space was never private.

Chapter 3.2

E14 How many fingers 3.2 (50%)

In the Ministry of Love, O’Brien tortures Winston until he will see as many fingers as the Party says 3.2 (50%).

Why it matters. Truth is replaced by obedience inside Winston’s mind.

Chapter 3.3

E15 Power for its own sake 3.3 (20–70%)

O’Brien explains that the Party seeks power for its own sake 3.3 (20%), that power is inflicting pain and humiliation 3.3 (45%), and offers his image of the future 3.3 (55%). Winston is the last man 3.3 (70%).

Why it matters. The novel’s darkest statement of what totalitarian power wants.

Chapter 3.5

E16 Room 101 3.5 (10–90%)

In Room 101 Winston faces the worst thing in the world 3.5 (15%), rats, and betrays Julia 3.5 (90%).

Why it matters. The last loyalty is broken.

Chapter 3.6

E17 The Chestnut Tree Café 3.6 (0–100%)

Released, Winston drinks gin, traces 2+2=5 in the dust 3.6 (30%), meets Julia who says she betrayed him 3.6 (45%), and ends loving Big Brother 3.6 (100%).

Why it matters. The novel ends in total defeat.

Dig deeper. Is there any hope in the ending, or in the Appendix that follows it?

Orwell’s methods

AO3: how the writer creates meanings and effects through language, structure and form.

Close third-person narration

Everything is seen through Winston, so the reader shares his partial knowledge and his surprise at the arrest 2.10 (50%).

Defamiliarisation

The first sentence makes a normal day strange 1.1 (0%).

Embedded documents

The diary, the slogans, Goldstein’s book 2.9 (10%) and the Appendix give the novel several voices.

Symbolic objects

The paperweight 1.8 (80%) and the rhyme 1.8 (75%) carry the lost past.

The Appendix

“The Principles of Newspeak” is written in the past tense App (0%), which some readers take as a sign that the regime did not last.

Dig deeper

The surface overview is in the event bank and issue arcs. These sections go further: the novel’s politics, language and thought, memory and history, the body and love, and the Appendix. Every claim about the novel is referenced; critical readings are offered for evaluation.

Power for its own sake

On the surface. The Party wants control.

O’Brien’s explanation strips power of any ideal 3.3 (20%). It could be argued that this is the novel’s most original claim: that totalitarianism is not a corrupted ideology but the pursuit of power itself 3.3 (45%).

Questions to explore
  • Is O’Brien telling the truth about the Party, or performing one more lie?

Language and thought

On the surface. The Party uses slogans.

Orwell’s essay “Politics and the English Language” (1946) argues that slovenly language makes foolish thoughts easier. Newspeak takes this to the extreme 1.5 (25%), and the novel tests whether thought can survive without words 1.7 (100%).

Questions to explore
  • Can a language make some thoughts impossible?

Memory, history and truth

On the surface. The Party rewrites records.

The memory hole 1.4 (5%) and the slogan about the past 1.3 (70%) make history a tool of power. It could be argued that Winston’s rebellion is fundamentally historical: an attempt to prove the past existed.

Questions to explore
  • Why does the Party care about the past at all?

The body, love and the limits of resistance

On the surface. Winston and Julia rebel.

Their rebellion is bodily and private 2.2 (100%); the Party breaks it through the body 3.5 (15%). Critics have debated whether Julia is a fully realised character or a projection of Winston’s desires.

Questions to explore
  • Why does the Party need to break love, not only stop rebellion?

The Appendix and the question of hope

On the surface. The novel ends in defeat.

The Appendix describes Newspeak in the past tense App (0%). Some readers, following Margaret Atwood, see this as a quiet sign that the regime eventually fell; others treat it as a scholarly convention.

Questions to explore
  • Does the Appendix change how you read the ending?
